\paragraph{Problem 2.} (p. 56, \#27)

Let $S$ be the set of rational numbers that can be written with odd denominator. Then $S$ is a subring of $\mathbb Q$ but not a field.

\begin{proof}
    Let $(p_1 / q_1), (p_2 / q_2) \in S$ be any rationals with odd denominator, such that $2 \nmid q_1$ and $2 \nmid q_2$. We can verify that $S$ is closed under addition and multiplication by computing their sums and products:
    \[ \frac{p_1}{q_1} + \frac{p_2}{q_2} = \frac{q_2 p_1 +  q_1 p_2}{q_1 q_2} \in S,
    \qquad\;
    \frac{p_1}{q_1} \cdot \frac{p_2}{q_2} = \frac{p_1 p_2}{q_1 q_2} \in S. 
    \] 

    Note that by Euclid's lemma, the product of two odd numbers is odd, so our denominators $q_1 q_2$ are odd, and our sums and products are in $S$. In $S$, we also find the additive and multiplicative identities:
    \[1 = \frac{1}{1} \in S, \qquad \qquad 0 = \frac{0}{1} \in S.\]
    Given any $p/q \in S$, the additive inverse $-p/q$ is also a member of $S$. Thus, $S$ is a subring of $\mathbb Q$ with identity. 
    
    However, $S$ is not a field. We can show that $2 \in S$ has no multiplicative inverse. Assume for contradiction that there exists some $p/q \in S$, with $2 \nmid q$, such that $2 \cdot p/q = 1$. Then, we would have that $q = 2p$, contradicting the fact that $2 \nmid q$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Problem 5.} (p. 57, \#40)
For all $d \in \mathbb Z$ that are not perfect squares, \[\mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}] = \left\{ a + b \sqrt{d} : a, b \in \mathbb Q \right\}\] is a subfield of $\mathbb C$.

\begin{proof}
    We consider $\mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}] \subseteq \mathbb C$, and note that it is obviously closed under addition and additive inverses as it is a rational vector subspace of $\mathbb C$. To show that it is closed under multiplication, consider any $x, x' \in \mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}]$ and write $x = a + b \sqrt{d}$ and $x' = a' + b' \sqrt{d}$. Then, \begin{align*} x\cdot x' &= a a' + a b' \sqrt{d} + b a' \sqrt{d} + b b' d \\ &= (a a' + b b' d) + (a b' + b a') \sqrt{d} \in \mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}].\end{align*}
    Because multiplication in $\mathbb C$ is commutative, multiplication on $\mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}]$ is also commutative. Finally, we can show the existence of an additive inverse for all nonzero $x = a + b \sqrt{d}$, given explicitly by \[ x^{-1} = \frac{a}{a^2 - b^2 d} + \frac{-b}{a^2 - b^2 d} \sqrt{d}. \]
    Since $d$ is not a perfect square, $b^2 d$ is also not a perfect square (unique prime factorization), and so $b^2 d \neq a^2$. Thus, since either $a$ or $b$ is nonzero, the denominator is nonzero, and we have $x^{-1} \in \mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}]$. Because multiplication is commutative, it suffices to compute $x \cdot x^{-1}$ as follows:
    \begin{align*}
        x \cdot x^{-1} = \frac{1}{a^2 - b^2d} \cdot (a^2 - ab \sqrt{d} + ba \sqrt{d} - b^2 d) = 1.
    \end{align*}
    $\mathbb Q[\sqrt{d}]$ is a subfield of $\mathbb C$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Problem 7.} Let $R$ be a ring. Then for all $a \in R$, $a \cdot 0 = 0$.

\begin{proof}
    By the property of $0$ as the additive identity, we know $0 + 0 = 0$. Thus, $a \cdot (0 + 0) = a \cdot 0$, and by left distributivity we get $a \cdot 0 + a \cdot 0 = a \cdot 0$. By the existence of additive inverses and the associativity of addition, we have some $-(a \cdot 0)$ which we can use to directly compute
    \begin{align*}
        a \cdot 0 + a \cdot 0 + -(a \cdot 0) &= a \cdot 0 + -(a\cdot 0) \\
        a \cdot 0 + 0 &= 0 \\ a \cdot 0 &= 0. &\qedhere
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Problem 8.} (p. 68, \#17) If $u$ is a unit in a ring $R$ with identity, then $u$ is not a zero divisor.

\begin{proof}
    Let $u^{-1} \in R$ be the element such that $u \cdot u^{-1} = u^{-1} \cdot u = 1$. Let $b \in R$ be any element such that $ub = 0$. Then, we must have that $u^{-1} u b = u^{-1} \cdot 0$. Using the same idea as \textbf{Problem 7} above, one can replace left multiplication with right multiplication to show that $u^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0$. By the associativity of multiplication, we get $u^{-1} u b = 1 \cdot b = b = 0$. Similarly, if $a \in R$ is any element such that $a u = 0$, then $a u u^{-1} = 0 \cdot u^{-1}$ and so $a \cdot 1 = a = 0$. Thus, the unit $u$ is not a zero-divisor.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Problem 10.} (p. 80, \#7)
$\mathbb R$ is isomorphic to the ring $S$ of all $2 \times 2$ matrices of the form $\begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{bmatrix}$ for $a \in \mathbb R$.

\begin{proof}
    Define $\varphi : \mathbb R \to S$ by $a \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{bmatrix}$. We wish to show that this is a bijective ring homomorphism. First, we can verify that $\varphi$ respects addition, as 
    \[ \varphi(a + b) = \begin{bmatrix} a + b & 0 \\ 0 & a + b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} b & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{bmatrix} = \varphi(a) + \varphi(b). \]
    Additionally, $\varphi$ also respects multiplication, as
    \[ \varphi(a \cdot b) = \begin{bmatrix} a \cdot b & 0 \\ 0 & a \cdot b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{bmatrix} = \varphi(a) \cdot \varphi(b). \]
    We can also verify that $\varphi$ is injective and surjective. Injectivity follows from the fact that \[\begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a' & 0 \\ 0 & a' \end{bmatrix} \iff a = a', \]
    and surjectivity follows from the definition of $S$, as it corresponds exactly to the image of $\varphi$. Thus, $\mathbb R$ and $S$ are isomorphic as rings.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Problem 11.} (p. 80, \#8)

The function $ f : \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2}] \to \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2}]$ given by $f(a + b\sqrt{2}) = a - b\sqrt{2}$ is a ring isomorphism.

\begin{proof}
    First, we can verify that $f$ respects addition and multiplication:
    \begin{align*}
     f\left(\left(a + b\sqrt{2}\right)+ \left(c + d\sqrt{2}\right)\right) &= (a + c) - (b + d)\sqrt{2} \\ &= \left(a - b\sqrt{2}\right) + \left(c - d\sqrt{2}\right) = f\left(a + b \sqrt{2}\right) + f\left(c + d\sqrt{2}\right). \\
    f\left(\left(a + b\sqrt{2}\right)\left(c + d \sqrt{2}\right)\right) &= (ac + 2bd) - (ad + bc)\sqrt{2} \\ &= \left(a - b \sqrt{2}\right) \left(c - d\sqrt{2}\right) = f\left(a + b\sqrt{2}\right)f\left(c + d\sqrt{2}\right). 
    \end{align*}
    Additionally, $f$ is surjective as for any $a + b\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2}]$, we have $f\left(a - b\sqrt{2}\right) = a + b\sqrt{2}$. Injectivity also follows from the fact that
    \[a + b\sqrt{2} = a' + b' \sqrt{2} \iff a = a' \land b = b' \iff a - b\sqrt{2} = a' - b'\sqrt{2}. \qedhere\]
\end{proof}
